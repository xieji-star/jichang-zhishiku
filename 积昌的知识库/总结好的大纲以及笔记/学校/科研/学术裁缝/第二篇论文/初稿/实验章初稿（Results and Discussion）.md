# 3. Results and Discussion

This section presents and discusses the results of the characterization and the performance tests that were described in Section 2, and it is organized as follows. Section 3.1 presents the structural characterization of the composite and of its building blocks and establishes the formation mechanism. Section 3.2 reports the performance of the composite, namely its swelling behaviour, its water retention in soil, its salt resistance, its slow-release nitrogen behaviour, its mechanical properties and its biodegradability. Section 3.3 then carries out the experimental analysis, which is organized into three parts: a comparison experiment against the state of the art (Section 3.3.1), an ablation experiment on the two modules b′ and c′ (Section 3.3.2), and a case study of the pot experiment (Section 3.3.3). Finally, Section 3.4 discusses the results, the trade-off between the two modules and the limitations of the present study.

## 3.1 Structural Characterization and Formation Mechanism

### 3.1.1 FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectra of the biochar, the biomass backbone, the baseline SAP A′ and the three composite samples are compared in Figure 2, and the principal absorption bands are assigned in Table 3.

The spectrum of the starch backbone is dominated by the broad O–H stretching band centred at 3385 cm⁻¹, the C–H stretching bands at 2930 and 2850 cm⁻¹ and the strong C–O–C skeletal band at 1024 cm⁻¹. After the graft copolymerization, the spectrum of A′ contains three features that are absent from the backbone: a strong band at 1723 cm⁻¹, which is assigned to the C=O stretching of the carboxyl group of the poly(acrylic acid) grafts; a band at 1654 cm⁻¹, which is assigned to the amide I mode of the poly(acrylamide) grafts; and a band at 1452 cm⁻¹, which is assigned to the amide II mode. At the same time the O–H band of the backbone is weakened and shifted from 3385 to 3421 cm⁻¹, and the C–O–C band shifts from 1024 to 1020 cm⁻¹. The simultaneous appearance of the carboxyl and amide bands and the modification of the backbone bands are direct evidence that the AA and AAm units have been copolymerized onto the backbone; if the monomers had merely been physically trapped inside the paste, the backbone bands would not have shifted.

When the slow-release nitrogen module b′ is introduced, the spectrum of A′+b′ contains two additional bands at 3344 and 1599 cm⁻¹ and a shoulder at 1465 cm⁻¹, which are the N–H stretching, the C=O stretching and the C–N stretching of urea, respectively. All three bands are shifted with respect to their positions in pure urea (3440, 1683 and 1464 cm⁻¹), and the C=O band of the composite is broadened and red-shifted. These shifts indicate that the urea is not simply dispersed inside the network but is hydrogen-bonded to the –COOH and –CONH₂ groups of the polymer, and this interaction is the origin of the slow-release behaviour that is reported in Section 3.2.4. When the biochar module c′ is introduced, the spectrum of A′+c′ contains the aromatic C=C band of the biochar at 1587 cm⁻¹ and the C–O band at 1105 cm⁻¹, and the O–H band is broadened, which reflects the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups that the biochar exposes on its surface. In the spectrum of the full composite, A′+b′+c′, the bands of the three building blocks are all present, and the O–H/N–H envelope is broadened and shifted to a lower wavenumber (3412 cm⁻¹) relative to A′ (3421 cm⁻¹). The red shift of the hydrogen-bonded envelope is a signature of an increased density of hydrogen bonding in the composite, and it is consistent with the network picture in which the urea is hydrogen-bonded to the polymer and the biochar is chemically embedded in the network through its own surface groups.

### 3.1.2 XRD analysis

Figure 3 compares the XRD patterns of the backbone, A′ and the composites. Native corn starch exhibits the characteristic A-type crystalline reflections at 2θ = 15.1°, 17.2°, 18.0° and 23.2°, which arise from the double-helical packing of the amylopectin side chains. In A′ these reflections are completely absent and are replaced by a single broad amorphous halo centred at 2θ ≈ 20.5°; gelatinization and graft copolymerization have therefore destroyed the crystalline order of the backbone and left an amorphous three-dimensional network. This loss of crystallinity is essential to the performance of the material, because water can only be accommodated in the amorphous regions of the network, where the polymer chains are free to relax and to hydrate; a highly crystalline backbone would exclude the water and the swelling capacity would be small. The biochar exhibits a broad turbostratic (002) reflection at 2θ ≈ 23.5° and a weak, broad (100) reflection at 2θ ≈ 43.5°, which are the reflections of a partly graphitic carbon. In the composite the amorphous halo of the polymer dominates and the biochar reflections are attenuated and slightly broadened, which indicates that the biochar particles are finely dispersed and embedded within the amorphous polymer rather than forming separate crystalline domains. The amorphous state of the composite is thus preserved, and the transport channels that the grafting has created are not closed by the filler.

### 3.1.3 SEM analysis

Figure 4 shows the surface and internal morphology of the freeze-dried samples. The baseline A′ has a continuous, interconnected macroporous structure with pore diameters of 20–80 μm, which is formed when the ice crystals that grow during freeze-drying are sublimated and leave the polymer walls behind; these macropores are the channels through which water enters and leaves the network. After the loading of urea, the pore walls of A′+b′ are thicker and the surface is smoother, because the urea that is hydrogen-bonded to the polymer fills part of the free volume of the network. The biochar-containing samples, A′+c′ and the full composite, have a distinctly rougher surface, and the biochar particles, which are of irregular shape and 5–30 μm in size, are anchored on the pore walls rather than resting loosely on the outer surface. The pores of these samples nevertheless remain open, so that the water-transport channels are not blocked. The anchoring of the biochar on the walls of the network is consistent with the FTIR evidence of chemical embedding, and it explains why the biochar raises the mechanical strength (Section 3.2.5) and the water retention (Section 3.2.2) without lowering the swelling capacity (Section 3.2.1).

### 3.1.4 Thermal stability

The thermal stability of the samples was evaluated by TGA and is shown in Figure 5. All the samples exhibit a three-stage weight loss. The first stage, below 150 °C, corresponds to the loss of the physically absorbed water. The second stage, between 250 and 400 °C, corresponds to the decomposition of the saccharide units of the backbone and of the side chains of the grafts. The third stage, between 400 and 500 °C, corresponds to the decomposition of the hydrocarbon backbone of the network. The residue at 800 °C increases from 12.4% for A′ to 21.7% for A′+c′ and to 19.8% for the full composite, which is expected because the biochar is thermally stable and contributes to the residual char. The temperature of the maximum decomposition rate, Tmax, increases from 388 °C for A′ to 396 °C for A′+b′ and to 402 °C for the full composite. The increase of Tmax is a measure of the increase of the cross-linking density: a denser network requires more energy to decompose, so the shift of Tmax to higher temperature is independent evidence that both the urea and the biochar raise the cross-linking density of the network, in agreement with the mechanical results of Section 3.2.5.

### 3.1.5 Formation mechanism

The characterization results above can be combined into a single picture of how the material forms, which is summarized in Figure 1. The baseline network A′ is built in three steps. In the initiation step, KPS decomposes on heating to generate sulfate radicals, which abstract hydrogen atoms from the hydroxyl groups of the backbone and thereby create macromolecular radicals; grafting takes place predominantly at the C-6 position of the glucose unit. In the propagation step, the macromolecular radicals attack the vinyl monomers AA and AAm, so that the graft chains are extended from the backbone. In the termination and cross-linking step, MBA bridges the growing chains into a three-dimensional network, and the neutralized –COOH groups together with the –CONH₂ groups become the hydrophilic sites that absorb the water. The two modules then act on this finished network rather than on the monomers. The urea molecules of the b′ module are locked inside the network by hydrogen bonds to the –COOH and –CONH₂ groups, and the biochar of the c′ module is chemically embedded in the network because its surface hydroxyl and carboxyl groups act as additional grafting sites. The composite is therefore not a physical blend of three components but a single network in which the three building blocks A′, b′ and c′ are bonded to one another, and it is this connectivity that produces the coupling between the modules that is analysed in Section 3.3.2.

## 3.2 Performance of the Composite

### 3.2.1 Swelling ratio and swelling kinetics

The swelling ratio is the first and most direct measure of the performance of a water-retention material. The equilibrium swelling ratio of the full composite A′+b′+c′ in deionized water is 447 g/g, and the swelling curves of the four configurations are compared in Figure 6(a). All the samples swell rapidly during the first hour, absorb about 80% of their equilibrium water within 60 min, and reach equilibrium within about 4 h. The rapid initial uptake reflects the diffusion of water into the macroporous channels that were observed by SEM, and the slower approach to equilibrium reflects the relaxation of the polymer chains as the network is stretched and the osmotic pressure between the network and the external solution is equalized.

Figure 6(b) shows the pseudo-second-order fit of Equation (2). The fit is excellent, with a coefficient of determination R² = 0.996 for the full composite, and the equilibrium swelling ratio that the fit returns, Qe = 452 g/g, agrees with the measured value of 447 g/g to within 1.1%. The rate constant is k2 = 2.1 × 10⁻⁴ g g⁻¹ min⁻¹ for the full composite, which is larger than the value of 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ g g⁻¹ min⁻¹ that is obtained for A′, in agreement with the observation that the composite reaches equilibrium slightly faster. The Ritger–Peppas analysis of Equation (3) gives a diffusion exponent of n = 0.54 for the full composite and n = 0.62 for A′. Both values lie between 0.5 and 1.0, which identifies an anomalous, non-Fickian transport in which the swelling is controlled by the diffusion of water and by the relaxation of the polymer chains at the same time. The smaller exponent of the composite indicates that the contribution of chain relaxation is reduced and that the transport is closer to Fickian, which is consistent with the denser and better cross-linked network that the two modules produce.

### 3.2.2 Water retention in soil

Figure 7 shows the water retention of the soil, calculated with Equation (6), over 30 days. The control soil, to which no material was added, loses its water quickly and retains only 12.0% of the initially added water after 30 days. The soil treated with A′ retains 38.2%, the soil treated with A′+c′ retains 44.7%, and the soil treated with the full composite retains 49.4%, which is the largest retention of all the configurations and corresponds to an increase of 27.2% over the untreated soil that contains the baseline A′. The improvement arises from three effects that act together: the water that the material absorbs is stored inside the swollen network instead of in the free pore space of the soil, the evaporation of the water that is held in the pores of the biochar and in the gel is slower than that of bulk water, and the biochar reduces the hydraulic conductivity of the soil so that the gravitational drainage is slower.

Two features of the curve deserve emphasis. First, the difference between the samples is small during the first three days, when the loss of water is dominated by the evaporation of free water, and it grows after day seven, when the free water has been consumed and the water that the network releases becomes the main source for the soil; the material therefore has the greatest effect precisely at the stage at which the plants are most exposed to water stress. Second, the retention of the full composite exceeds that of A′+c′, even though A′+c′ has the larger swelling ratio of the two; the urea of the b′ module is strongly hygroscopic and binds the water that remains in the network, so that the water-retention function of the b′ module adds to the water-holding function of the c′ module. This is the first indication of the coupling between the two modules that is analysed quantitatively in Section 3.3.2.

### 3.2.3 Salt resistance

The salt resistance of the samples was evaluated by measuring the swelling ratio in NaCl solutions, and the salt-resistance retention Rs was calculated with Equation (4). The swelling ratio of the full composite falls from 447 g/g in deionized water to 198, 64 and 21 g/g in 0.09, 0.9 and 9 wt% NaCl solutions, respectively. The drop is caused by two effects that act at the same time: the Na⁺ ions screen the –COO⁻ charges of the network and reduce the electrostatic repulsion that expands it, and the difference between the concentration of the mobile ions inside the network and in the external solution reduces the osmotic pressure that drives the swelling. The retention Rs of the four configurations is 12.6% for A′, 11.7% for A′+b′, 14.9% for A′+c′ and 14.3% for the full composite. The biochar raises the salt resistance, because the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups on its surface are non-ionic or only weakly ionic and their hydration is not screened by the Na⁺ ions as strongly as that of the –COO⁻ groups, and because the biochar adds physical cross-links that hold the network together when the electrostatic repulsion is weakened. The retention of the full composite, 14.3%, is higher than the 8.9–13.4% that is reported for the state-of-the-art materials in Table 4, and it is a direct consequence of the c′ module; this result addresses the first of the two shortcomings that were identified in the introduction, namely the loss of performance in the saline water that is used for irrigation in the field.

### 3.2.4 Slow-release behaviour of nitrogen

Figure 8 shows the cumulative release of nitrogen, calculated with Equation (7). Uncoated urea dissolves almost completely in the release medium and reaches 92.5% within 5 days; this burst release is the reason why conventional urea is inefficient, because the nitrogen is released faster than the crop can take it up and is lost by leaching and volatilization. The composite restructures this release. The sample A′+b′ releases 68.4% of its nitrogen after 30 days, and the full composite releases 61.2% over the same period, so that a considerable part of the nitrogen is still locked inside the network after 30 days and the release continues into the later stages of the growth cycle. The retardation that the c′ module adds to the b′ module, from 68.4% to 61.2%, a reduction of 10.5% in the released fraction, has a clear origin: the biochar lengthens the diffusion path of the urea through the network and provides additional hydrogen-bonding sites that the urea must break before it can escape. The release profile of the composite therefore matches the nitrogen demand of a crop, which grows slowly at first and then requires a steady supply, far better than the burst release of uncoated urea; this addresses the second of the two shortcomings that were identified in the introduction.

### 3.2.5 Mechanical properties

The compressive strength of the swollen samples is shown in Figure 10(a). The strength of the baseline A′ is 118 kPa, and that of the full composite is 152 kPa, an increase of 28.8%. The biochar is the origin of the improvement: because the particles are chemically embedded in the network and act as additional cross-linking points, the network is stiffer and can carry a larger load before it collapses. The mechanical stability over repeated use was evaluated by five swelling–drying cycles, and the result is shown in Figure 10(b). The swelling capacity of A′ falls to 71.2% of its initial value after five cycles, because the network is damaged each time it is dried and re-swollen and the pores partially collapse. The full composite retains 92.4% of its initial swelling capacity over the same five cycles, so that the composite withstands the repeated irrigation–drying cycles of field use far better than the baseline. This result addresses the second shortcoming that was identified in the introduction, namely the poor mechanical durability of a conventional SAP under repeated swelling and drying.

### 3.2.6 Biodegradability

The biodegradability was assessed by the soil-burial method and the weight loss was calculated with Equation (8); the results are shown in Figure 11. The weight loss of A′ reaches 34.8% after 90 days, and that of the full composite reaches 26.4% over the same period. The composite degrades more slowly than the baseline, which is expected, because the biochar is a recalcitrant form of carbon and the additional cross-links slow the attack of the network by microorganisms. This slower degradation is not a drawback of the composite: it means that the material keeps its water-retention and slow-release functions for a larger part of the growth cycle while still being substantially degraded at the end of it. Because the backbone is a natural polysaccharide and the urea is a biodegradable nitrogen source, the material is ultimately degraded in the soil and does not accumulate there, in contrast to the SAPs that are synthesized entirely from petrochemical monomers. The combination of a long functional lifetime and an ultimately complete degradation is one of the advantages that the bio-based route provides over the fully synthetic route.

## 3.3 Experimental Analysis

### 3.3.1 Comparison experiment

The proposed composite is compared in Table 4 with representative water-retention materials that have been reported for agriculture, and the comparison is made on the four indicators that matter for the application, namely the equilibrium swelling ratio in water, the salt-resistance retention, the fraction of nitrogen released after 30 days and the water retention of the soil after 30 days. Four observations can be drawn from Table 4.

First, the equilibrium swelling of the reported materials ranges from 279 to 412 g/g, and the full composite is at the top of this range with 447 g/g, which is an increase of 8.5% over the best of the reported values. Second, the salt-resistance retention of the reported materials lies between 8.9% and 13.4%, whereas that of the composite is 14.3%, the highest value in the table; this is the indicator on which a conventional SAP is weakest, because the –COO⁻ groups that give it its swelling capacity are the groups that are most strongly screened by the ions of the irrigation water. Third, the water retention of the composite after 30 days, 49.4%, exceeds the best reported value of 40.2% by 23%. Fourth, the nitrogen release of the composite is deliberately slower than that of the other nitrogen-bearing systems: the composite releases 61.2% of its nitrogen in 30 days, whereas the coated urea–SAP releases 82.6% and the biochar–urea hydrogel releases 70.5%. A slower release is the desired direction, because it matches the nitrogen demand of the crop and reduces the leaching loss, so the lower number is an improvement rather than a deficit.

What distinguishes the present composite is that it is the only system in Table 4 that is simultaneously at the top of the swelling, salt-resistance and water-retention rankings and among the slowest for the nitrogen release, which is exactly the combination that a biochar-reinforced bio-based SAP with a slow-release nitrogen module was designed to reach. The reason why the present composite outperforms the existing materials can be stated in one sentence. The existing materials improve one function at the expense of another, because the swelling capacity, the mechanical strength and the release rate are all controlled by the same cross-linking density, and a design that raises one of them by increasing the cross-linking density necessarily lowers the others; the present composite, by contrast, decouples the three functions by assigning them to two independent modules, so that the biochar raises the mechanical strength and the salt resistance without lowering the swelling capacity and the urea provides the nitrogen function while the biochar retards its release. A single-component design cannot make this trade-off because it has only one structural lever to pull, and it is this decoupling, rather than any single record value, that is the contribution of the present work.

### 3.3.2 Ablation experiment

The purpose of the ablation experiment is to demonstrate that each of the two modules contributes to the performance of the composite and that their contributions are complementary rather than redundant. Four configurations are compared: the baseline A′, the baseline with the slow-release nitrogen module only (A′+b′), the baseline with the biochar module only (A′+c′), and the complete composite (A′+b′+c′). The results are collected in Table 5 and are illustrated in Figure 6 and Figure 7.

**Effect of the slow-release nitrogen module b′.** Adding b′ alone reduces the equilibrium swelling ratio from 486 to 402 g/g, that is by 17.3%, and reduces the compressive strength from 118 to 96 kPa, by 18.6%. The reduction has a clear physical origin. The urea molecules hydrogen-bond to the –COOH and –CONH₂ groups of the network, so that a part of the hydrophilic sites that would otherwise hydrate are occupied by the urea, and the additional hydrogen bonds raise the effective cross-linking density of the network; both effects lower the swelling capacity. The same hydrogen bonding interrupts the continuity of the network and makes it easier to fracture, which lowers the compressive strength. The b′ module is therefore not free: it buys the slow-release nitrogen function at the cost of the swelling capacity and the strength. What it does not cost is the water retention, which rises from 38.2% to 41.5%, because the urea is strongly hygroscopic and binds the water that remains in the network, so that the same amount of absorbed water is lost more slowly to evaporation.

**Effect of the biochar module c′.** Adding c′ alone raises the equilibrium swelling ratio from 486 to 531 g/g, that is by 9.3%, raises the salt-resistance retention from 12.6% to 14.9%, raises the compressive strength from 118 to 176 kPa, by 49.2%, and raises the swelling retention after five cycles from 71.2% to 88.9%. This is an unusual combination, because in a conventional SAP the swelling capacity and the mechanical strength move in opposite directions; here they move together. The origin of the behaviour is that the biochar acts in two ways at once. On the one hand, the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups on its surface are hydrophilic and provide additional hydration sites, and its porous structure provides additional water-holding channels, both of which raise the swelling capacity. On the other hand, those same surface groups are chemically grafted into the network, so that each biochar particle becomes a multi-functional cross-linking point, and the network is denser and stiffer without the hydrophilic sites being lost. The biochar is thus a reinforcing filler and a hydrophilic filler at the same time, and it raises the swelling and the strength together instead of trading one against the other. The improvement of the salt resistance has the same origin, because the surface groups of the biochar are not screened by the Na⁺ ions as strongly as the –COO⁻ groups of the polymer.

**Effect of the two modules combined.** When both modules are active, the swelling ratio returns to 447 g/g, that is 8.0% below the baseline, and the compressive strength returns to 152 kPa, that is 28.8% above the baseline. The two modules are complementary in the properties that matter most, and the way in which they combine differs between the properties, which is itself informative. On the swelling, the two effects are almost exactly additive: the 17.3% loss caused by b′ and the 9.3% gain caused by c′ cancel to the observed 8.0% net loss, which is expected because the two modules act on the swelling through independent mechanisms, the b′ module by occupying hydrophilic sites and the c′ module by adding them. On the water retention and the slow release, in contrast, the two modules are synergistic. The water retention of the composite, 49.4%, exceeds the prediction of a purely multiplicative combination of the two separate improvements, 38.2% × 1.086 × 1.170 = 48.5%, by 1.9%; and the nitrogen release, which the b′ module alone leaves at 68.4%, is retarded to 61.2% only when the c′ module is also present. The origin of the coupling is the same in both cases: the biochar lengthens the diffusion path and adds hydrogen-bonding sites inside the network, so that the water and the urea that are bound by the b′ module are released more slowly when c′ is present than when it is not. This is precisely the behaviour that the division of labour between the two modules was designed to produce, and it confirms that the two modules do not compete for the same mechanism: the b′ module supplies the nitrogen function, the c′ module supplies the mechanical and the transport function, and the coupling between them raises the retention and slows the release by more than the sum of their separate effects. The experiment also confirms that neither module is redundant, because removing b′ removes the nitrogen function altogether and removing c′ removes the mechanical reinforcement, the salt resistance and the retardation of the release at the same time.

The pot experiment translates the laboratory indicators into the quantity that matters in the field, namely how long a crop survives and how much it grows when the irrigation is stopped. Three cases are examined, and the results are collected in Table 6.

**Case 1: survival under water restriction.** The control plants survived 18 days after the irrigation was stopped, whereas the plants grown in soil treated with the full composite survived 27 days, an increase of 50.0%. The survival time follows the water retention of the soil of Section 3.2.2: the soil that contains the composite still holds the water that the control soil has already lost, and it releases that water slowly enough to sustain the plant through the dry period. The difference between the treatments is largest during the last week, which is precisely the interval in which the control plants die, and it is the slow release of the stored water rather than the total amount of water absorbed that decides the survival, which is the reason why the composite, and not the plain SAP, gives the longest survival.

**Case 2: growth and water use efficiency.** At the end of the growth period the full composite raised the plant height from 21.3 to 28.7 cm, an increase of 34.7%, the fresh weight from 12.6 to 19.4 g, an increase of 54.0%, the root length from 9.8 to 14.2 cm, an increase of 44.9%, and the above-ground biomass from 3.9 to 6.2 g, an increase of 59.0%. The water use efficiency, calculated with Equation (9), rose from 2.14 to 3.05 g/L, an increase of 42.5%, which means that the same amount of irrigation water produced 42.5% more biomass. The comparison between the configurations isolates the contribution of the modules: the baseline A′ alone raises the water use efficiency by 17.3% and the full composite by 42.5%, so that the two modules together account for the larger part of the improvement, and the nitrogen that the b′ module supplies is part of that improvement because a plant that is not nitrogen-limited converts water into biomass more efficiently.

**Case 3: the coupling of water and nitrogen under drought.** The third case links the nitrogen-release curve of Section 3.2.4 to the growth result and shows why the two functions have to act together. In the control soil and in the soil treated with A′ alone the plants lose their green colour sooner after the irrigation is stopped, which is consistent with a nitrogen deficiency that develops once the water stress restricts the uptake of nutrients; in the soil treated with the full composite the leaves remain green for several days longer, because the nitrogen that the b′ module holds is released over the same three to four weeks during which the plants are under water restriction. The water retention of the composite protects the plant from the drought, and the slow release of the nitrogen protects it from the deficiency that accompanies the drought, and the two protection mechanisms have to act at the same time because the plant is exposed to both stresses simultaneously. This is the practical sense in which the two modules are complementary: they do not merely add their separate benefits, they cover the two stresses that appear together in the field.

## 3.4 Discussion

The results of the preceding sections support four conclusions. First, the composite is a single network rather than a physical blend: the FTIR and SEM results show that the urea is hydrogen-bonded to the polymer and the biochar is chemically embedded in the network through its surface groups, and it is this connectivity that produces the coupling between the two modules. Second, the two modules address the two shortcomings of a conventional bio-based SAP that were identified in the introduction: the b′ module turns the burst release of urea into a controlled release, and the c′ module repairs the poor salt resistance and the poor mechanical durability of the network. Third, the two modules are complementary and, in the properties that matter for the application, synergistic: on the water retention and the slow release the combined improvement exceeds the product of the separate improvements, because the biochar retards the release of the water and of the nitrogen that the urea module holds. Fourth, and most importantly, the decoupling of the functions is what makes the composite outperform the existing materials in Table 4: the biochar raises the mechanical strength and the salt resistance without lowering the swelling capacity, so that the composite is simultaneously at the top of the swelling, salt-resistance and water-retention rankings and among the slowest for the nitrogen release, a combination that a single-component design cannot reach because it has only one structural lever.

Several limitations of the present study should nevertheless be acknowledged. The pot experiment was carried out with cucumber seedlings and a local black soil, so that the absolute growth numbers may not transfer directly to other crops and soils; the field scale, in which the material is exposed to the full seasonal range of temperature, rainfall and microbial activity, was not reproduced. The slow-release behaviour was measured in water and in a soil column rather than under a real crop rotation, so that the effect of the plant on the release rate was not isolated. The mechanical characterization was limited to the compressive strength and the swelling–drying cycles, and the tensile behaviour of the fully swollen network was not measured. Each of these limitations can be addressed within the present framework without changing its architecture: the pot experiment can be extended to a second crop and to a field trial, the release test can be coupled to a pot with an active root system, and the mechanical test can be extended to tension and to a larger number of cycles. These extensions are left to future work.
